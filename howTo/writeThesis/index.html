How to write your thesis

This page explains how your M.Sc. thesis should be organized: format, length and what goes in each section. Read it before you start writing, and again before you send me a draft.

Format and template

The thesis must be written in LaTeX, and the final document is a PDF. You can choose any template you like. Politecnico di Milano provides templates in several formats: I recommend the article format. The only exception is the title page, which can also be produced in the book format, so that it can be used if you decide to print the thesis as a classic bound copy.

Length

The thesis must not exceed 65 pages. Going beyond this limit is acceptable only because of blank pages or long appendices. None of the M.Sc. theses I propose requires more than 65 pages.

Overall structure

The thesis is divided into chapters (book format) or sections (article format). Subchapters and subsections are rarely useful, so avoid them.

After the abstract in English and the abstract in your native language (each followed by keywords in the same language), the thesis develops as follows:

  1. Introduction
  2. Governing equations
  3. Numerical modeling
  4. Problem description
  5. Validation and verification
  6. Results
  7. Conclusions

followed by the references and, if needed, the appendices.

1. Introduction

The introduction leads the reader into the topic of the thesis. It must not contain unnecessary information: it contains only the information that is essential for what follows. In particular, the introduction holds at least 90% of all the references cited in the thesis, which are typically 35–45.

The introduction is a single, continuous text: one fluent discussion in which each paragraph leads naturally to the next. It has no internal headings, no labels and no numbered parts. The steps below describe the logical thread the text should follow, not titles to copy into your thesis: do not write “Framing”, “Literature”, “Motivations”, etc. in the introduction.

The thread usually goes like this:

  1. The first few lines (3–4) frame the problem in general terms (what we are talking about), and move from the general problem to the specific one.
  2. The text then describes what other authors have done and what is therefore available in the literature. Do not dwell on basic concepts, unnecessary explanations or topics loosely related to your thesis. For example, explaining the operating cycle of an aircraft engine is of little interest to a committee (and later readers) who know it well: refer to a textbook in half a sentence, and use the space for interesting details closely related to your topic. Reviewing the literature is where you reach the number of references above.
  3. The literature review leads to the scientific question, which must be stated clearly to the reader. It is the focus of the thesis, the reason why the work is carried out: what you want to show, demonstrate, obtain, design, program, etc.
  4. A paragraph explains the motivations, i.e., what drives the work: often a gap in the literature or a long-term goal (“this research lays the foundations for…”).
  5. A paragraph anticipates the highlights, i.e., the most important findings of the thesis. It is usually completed at the end of the work.
  6. A final paragraph describes how the rest of the thesis is organized: what is in Section 2, then 3, and so on up to the conclusions.

Do not use bullet points in the introduction. Inline enumerations are acceptable, for example: a) a list with letters; 1) a list with numbers.

2. Governing equations

This section presents the equations that govern the problems studied. Only the equations: no references to codes, methods, tools, software or solvers. If the problem relies on the compressible Navier–Stokes equations, there is no need to derive them.

3. Numerical modeling

This section describes all the numerical models and schemes used to solve the problem.

  • Do not use keywords of the CFD software you used.
  • For the numerical schemes, stating their order is enough.
  • For existing models (turbulence, combustion or others), give a citation instead of repeating the explanation that is already available in the literature.
  • New models, implemented by you or not yet available in the literature, must be explained in detail, and only in this section.

4. Problem description

This section describes the problem in detail, using sketches, images and plots. Include a summary table of the operating conditions and/or boundary conditions, without software keywords: for example, do not write fixedValue, write “imposed velocity” and give the value for the corresponding boundary.

This section also reports the grid size, the average mesh size and the total simulated time (end time).

Finally, describe here where and what you extract from the simulations to produce the plots shown later in the validation and results.

This does not mean showing the post-processing code. No scripts, no Python, no software dictionaries, no lists of commands or utilities. What is needed is:

  • in the figures of the geometry: the axial lines, the cutting planes and the probe locations drawn and labelled (e.g., “lines L1–L3”, “plane P1”, “probes A–D”);
  • in the text: a sentence saying what is extracted there and how, for example: “Mean and RMS axial velocity profiles are extracted along lines L1–L3 (x/D = 1, 2 and 5), averaging over 20 flow-through times. Pressure is sampled at probes A–D at 1 MHz to compute the spectra shown in Section 6.”

The goal is that the reader, looking at a plot in the results, knows exactly where the data come from. The code stays in your Git repository, not in the thesis.

5. Validation and verification

Before showing results, prove that they can be trusted. Keep the two parts separate:

  • Verification (are the equations solved correctly?): a grid sensitivity study on at least three grids, comparing the key quantities and justifying the grid you chose. For unsteady simulations, also show that the time step is adequate and that the statistics are converged (state the averaging window). If you implemented a new model, test it first on a simple case with a known reference solution.
  • Validation (are the right equations solved?): compare your results with experimental or reference numerical data, extracted at the lines, planes and probes defined in Section 4. Cite the source of the reference data, quantify the discrepancies and briefly discuss where they come from.

If no reference data exist for your configuration, say so explicitly and explain what you did instead (e.g., validation on a similar, well-documented configuration).

6. Results

This is the core of the thesis: the results answer the scientific question stated in the introduction.

  • Organize the results by topic, following the logic of the scientific question, not in the chronological order in which you ran the simulations.
  • Every figure must be cited and commented in the text: first say what it shows, then explain the physics behind it. A figure that is not commented should not be there.
  • Figures must be readable: axis labels with units, font size comparable to the text, consistent colors, colormaps and ranges when comparing cases.
  • Few citations here: use them only to compare your findings with the literature.

7. Conclusions

Keep the conclusions short (one to two pages). Restate the scientific question, summarize the approach in a few lines, list the main findings (quantitatively, where possible) and give a clear answer to the question. Close with the limitations of the work and possible future developments.

No new results, figures or references in the conclusions. Once they are written, go back to the introduction and make sure the highlights are consistent with them.

A note on appendices

Appendices are optional and host material that would interrupt the reading of the main text, for example:

  • long derivations;
  • additional data from the grid sensitivity study or from parametric cases;
  • large tables (e.g., kinetic mechanisms, thermophysical properties);
  • a summary of the settings needed to reproduce the simulations (the full case setup is in your Git repository).

The main text must remain complete without them: the appendices support it, they are not the place for results that matter.
